> Use artistic composition and environmental cues to guide players smoothly through your game.

{% include clip.liquid src="/assets/video/articles/how-to-use-composition-and-cues-to-guide-the-player/tombraider.mp4" caption="Shadow of the Tomb Raider paints climbable walls and jungle paths." %}

In Shadow of the Tomb Raider, the level designers use painted areas to lead the player along walls and through the jungle.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="/assets/img/articles/how-to-use-composition-and-cues-to-guide-the-player/lastofuslight.webp" class="img-fluid rounded" alt="Joel carries Sarah across a dark rooftop in The Last of Us toward a lit gap in the wall." caption="Light marks the way forward in The Last of Us." %}

Light is one of the strongest ways to guide players through a level. The Last of Us does this well: shiny objects usually point in the direction the designers want players to go.

{% include clip.liquid src="/assets/video/articles/how-to-use-composition-and-cues-to-guide-the-player/uncharted4-ani.mp4" caption="Boards and lanterns hang at swinging distance in Uncharted." %}

The arrangement of prefabs in Uncharted levels always hints at where to go next. In the clip above, the boards and lanterns sit at exactly the right swinging distance.

{% include figure.liquid loading="lazy" path="/assets/img/articles/how-to-use-composition-and-cues-to-guide-the-player/weenie.webp" class="img-fluid rounded" alt="The Citadel tower looms over City 17 in Half-Life 2." caption="The Citadel in Half-Life 2 is a weenie: a landmark that pulls players across the city." %}

Many games use weenies, a term from Walt Disney Imagineering for distant landmarks that draw people toward them. The Citadel in Half-Life 2 is a famous example. A weenie foreshadows something exciting that the player will reach later, and it keeps them moving toward it.
